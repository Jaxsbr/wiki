# Incremental Games: Core Loop & Time

Part of the [Incremental Games](incremental-games-overview.md) research set.

---

## The Canonical Incremental Loop

```
PRODUCE → ACCUMULATE → SPEND → UPGRADE → PRODUCE FASTER
```

Every incremental game is a variation on this cycle. The genre's art is in *how* each step is dressed, paced, and layered.

### Step-by-step breakdown

**1. Produce**
Resources are generated by the player (clicking), by buildings/generators (idle), or by both. Early game almost always starts with active clicking — even in idle-heavy games — so the player understands what the generator represents before the generator replaces them.

**2. Accumulate**
Resources pile up visibly. The counter ticking upward is itself satisfying. This is intentional: the genre discovered that making accumulation *visible* and *legible* is more engaging than hiding it. Cookie Clicker's giant cookie counter and CpS readout are primary UI elements, not secondary stats.

**3. Spend**
Resources are exchanged for upgrades, buildings, or unlocks. The spend decision is where the deepest strategic layer lives: which upgrade next? Should I save for the bigger one or buy now?

**4. Upgrade → Produce Faster**
Upgrades increase production rate — either linearly (flat +CpS), multiplicatively (×2 to a category), or exponentially (new tier that generates generators). Each upgrade shifts the loop's tempo.

## "Numbers Going Up" Is the Mechanic

In most games, numbers are feedback about progress. In incrementals, the rising number *is* the progress. The genre owes this insight to Progress Quest (2002), which proved players would watch an automated bar fill without any input at all — just to see what happened next.

Cookie Clicker designer Orteil has called his games "non-games" — but the compulsive engagement proves that watching a counter accelerate satisfies something real. Academic analysis frames it as:

- **Variable ratio reinforcement** (unpredictable reward timing keeps engagement high)
- **Dopamine anticipation loop**: Action → Reward → "Just a bit more until the next upgrade" → Repeat
- **Mathematical appreciation**: exponentially accelerating numbers feel like a small discovery each time

## Time as a Core Resource

In most games, time is the medium. In incrementals, **time is the cost**. Wait times are the primary friction — the equivalent of health points or stamina bars.

This has structural implications:

| Time role | Example |
|-----------|---------|
| **Early game cost**: wait 10 seconds to afford next building | Cookie Clicker cursor → grandma |
| **Mid-game cost**: wait 20 minutes for the next tier | Cookie Clicker shipment |
| **Prestige signal**: growth so slow that 1 hour = 0.01% of next upgrade | The "wall" — time to reset |
| **Offline resource**: time accumulates as production while away | AdVenture Capitalist, Egg Inc. |

Designing around time-as-cost means:
- Each new tier should feel like a meaningful amount of time has passed (not too fast = no appreciation; not too slow = boredom)
- Cookie Clicker's buildings each cost ~5× the previous; income per tier increases by ~1/3 — calibrated so players dwell in each tier long enough to enjoy it

## Active vs. Passive Income

Most incrementals support two modes simultaneously:

**Active income**: generated by clicking. High rate but requires attention.
**Passive income**: generated by buildings/automation. Lower initial rate but continuous.

The balance shifts over the game's arc:
- **Early**: active dominates. Clicking is the only income. This teaches the player what they're building toward.
- **Mid**: passive catches up. Buildings accumulate. Clicking becomes a boost, not a necessity.
- **Late**: passive completely dominates. Active clicking may still provide a bonus (typically 1–5% of passive rate), but is no longer required.

Well-designed games don't punish idleness or punish active play — they reward both, just differently.

**Active player advantage**: may access mini-games (Cookie Clicker's garden, stock market), golden cookie clicks, click-burst upgrades.
**Idle player advantage**: offline production, lower cognitive load, lower session frequency.

## Offline Progress

When the player leaves and returns, the game calculates:

```
gained = production_per_second × elapsed_seconds
```

This "delta-time" calculation fast-forwards game state without running a background server. The math is applied on login.

**Design decisions around offline progress:**

| Decision | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| **Cap offline hours** (e.g. 8h cap) | Encourages regular check-ins; prevents single session from fully "solving" the game |
| **No cap** | Reduces anxiety; rewards long breaks; less monetization pressure |
| **Offline efficiency penalty** (e.g. 50% rate) | Incentivizes active play; common in mobile F2P |
| **Offline bonus** (e.g. return bonus popup) | Makes returning feel rewarding; reduces guilt about absences |

Egg, Inc. caps offline at 2 hours by default (premium extends this), which is explicitly designed to encourage either active play or premium purchase.

**Why offline progress matters to design:**
1. Reduces anxiety ("I'm falling behind") — key for retention
2. Makes returning feel rewarding vs. punishing
3. Lets the game fit into a life, not demand it
4. Creates natural FOMO loop: "Come back to see what you earned"
